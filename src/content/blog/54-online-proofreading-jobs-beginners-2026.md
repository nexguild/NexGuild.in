## What Proofreading Work Involves

Proofreading is the final-stage review of written content — checking for:
- **Spelling errors** — including correctly spelled words used in the wrong context
- **Grammar errors** — subject-verb agreement, tense consistency, pronoun reference
- **Punctuation errors** — missing or misplaced commas, apostrophe misuse, inconsistent dash style
- **Formatting inconsistencies** — heading styles, spacing, font usage in documents
- **Typos** — characters transposed, words duplicated, missing words

Proofreading is not the same as editing — you're not restructuring sentences or improving argument clarity. You're catching surface-level errors in a document that's already been written and edited.

## Platform Reviews: Where to Find Proofreading Jobs

### Scribbr
Scribbr is a Dutch company that provides proofreading and editing services for academic documents — theses, dissertations, papers. They hire English-speaking editors globally.

**How to apply:** Submit an application and complete a paid sample edit. Scribbr pays for the sample regardless of whether you're hired.

**Pay:** Scribbr editors earn $0.02–$0.03 per word for proofreading tasks, equivalent to roughly $15–$25/hr for efficient readers.

**Best for:** People with academic writing familiarity who can work with research papers, theses, and formal academic English.

### ProofreadingPal
ProofreadingPal works with academic, business, and general documents. They use a two-editor system — every document is reviewed by two proofreaders.

**How to apply:** Submit a resume and complete a proofreading test (unpaid). Successful applicants receive work assignments.

**Pay:** Typically $1,000–$3,000/month for full-time-equivalent hours according to published contractor reports.

**Best for:** People who prefer structured work assignments rather than bidding for individual clients.

### Upwork
Upwork has a substantial market for proofreaders, ranging from academic to marketing copy to legal documents. Starting rates for beginners with no reviews are typically $12–$18/hr; rates rise quickly with reviews.

**How to apply:** Create a profile, pass Upwork's English proficiency test (optional but recommended), and start bidding on proofreading jobs.

**Pay:** $12–$50/hr depending on specialization and review history.

**Best for:** People who want to work with diverse client types and build a freelance portfolio.

### Fiverr
Fiverr's gig model is well-suited to proofreading because proofreading services are standardized — you can create a clear gig ("I will proofread up to 1,000 words for $15"). Clients browse and order without proposals.

**How to apply:** Create a Fiverr account and list a focused proofreading gig.

**Pay:** Set by you. Starting rates of $10–$25 for short documents are common for new sellers; experienced sellers charge $40–$100+ per document.

**Best for:** People who prefer the gig model over bidding and want clients to come to them.

## How to Pass Proofreading Platform Tests

Most proofreading platforms use tests to screen applicants. The tests vary but typically present a document with 10–30 intentional errors and ask you to identify and correct them.

**What tests cover:**
- Grammar (subject-verb agreement, tense consistency, modifiers)
- Punctuation (comma rules, semicolon usage, apostrophes)
- Spelling (commonly confused words: affect/effect, their/there/they're)
- Style consistency (British vs. American spelling, capitalization conventions)
- Formatting (bullet point consistency, heading hierarchy)

**How to prepare:**
1. Download the Chicago Manual of Style or AP Stylebook (the two most common style references) and review their core rules
2. Practice with free online proofreading exercises (search "proofreading test practice" for graded exercises)
3. Take your time on tests — most allow you to review your work before submitting; use that time

**Common mistakes on tests:**
- Missing correctly spelled but wrong-context words (form vs. from, trail vs. trial)
- Overlooking punctuation outside of commas (semicolons, en-dashes, em-dashes)
- Applying American rules to a British English document or vice versa
- Rushing the second half of the test (attention tends to drop)
